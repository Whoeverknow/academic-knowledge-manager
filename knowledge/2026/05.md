### P1-ACAD-EEM-0000000003 | Global Earth-economy Model: Integrating Ecosystem Services into CGE

**Created**: 2026-05-27 | **Updated**: 2026-05-27
**Priority**: P1 | **Tags**: `#world-bank` `#ecosystem-services` `#cge` `#integrated-assessment` `#t-method` `#st-new`
**Sources**:

  └ [Secondary] [Literature] Corong, E. L. et al. (2017). The Standard GTAP Model, Version 7. *Journal of Global Economic Analysis*, 2(1), 1-119.
  └ [Secondary] [Web] Full report → https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/35882

**Cite**:
- CORONG E L, et al. The standard GTAP model, version 7[J]. Journal of Global Economic Analysis, 2017, 2(1): 1-119.
- CERVIGNI R, et al. The economic case for nature[R]. Washington, D.C.: World Bank, 2021.

**Note**: 引文残缺 — Cervigni presentation (slide deck, Sep 2021) lacks formal publication details (page numbers, report number); the full World Bank report is accessible via the openknowledge handle cited above.

> A novel integrated framework linking 4 ecosystem services (pollination, timber, fisheries, carbon) to a global CGE model to assess the economic implications of nature loss and policy responses.

**Core Points**:
- **Three-layer architecture**: (1) Standard GTAP CGE model (141 regions × 65 sectors) without ecosystem services, (2) Ecosystem Services Model for 4 services, (3) Natural Capital module bridging the two
- **GTAP-AEZ (Agro-Ecological Zones) model** adds land competition across crops, pasture, and forestry with heterogeneous land endowments within each region; land rental rate determined by supply-demand equilibrium
- **Three analytical questions**: (Q1) What is the economic cost of ecosystem service collapse? (stress test), (Q2) Are there win-win nature-smart policies? (policy simulation), (Q3) Who wins and who loses? (distributional analysis)
- **Policy levers modeled**: Fiscal reform, Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES) expansion, agricultural intensification, trade policies
- **Model outputs**: GDP, welfare, factor use (unskilled labor, skilled labor, capital, land)
- **30×30 assessment method**: Determine BAU land use → optimized conservation under 30×30 → net effect = improved ES provision − lost production value; 5-arc-minute grid resolution (~8,605 ha/cell)
- **Spatial resolution limited by national economic accounts**; GTAP v7 standard
- **Note**: This entry captures the conceptual framework only; the presentation (slide deck) does not disclose equations, calibration parameters, or production functions. Full methodology in the underlying World Bank report.

  - P5 (P1+P3): 28% avoided conversion

  - P6 (P1+R&D): +$141.7B, 21% avoided conversion
  - P7 (P1+P3+R&D): +$147.9B, 50% avoided conversion ⬅ most effective single package
- **Forest carbon payments alone** reduce natural land loss by 26–35% (12–16M ha)

**C. 30×30 target**
- Without carbon co-benefits: −$115B (−0.10% global GDP)
- With carbon mitigation co-benefits: −$13B (−0.01%) → negligible net cost
- Uneven regional burden; need to mobilize resources in low-income economies

**D. Political economy**
- Nature-smart policies benefit labor wages but negatively impact land returns
- Country-level adoption depends on reconciling incentives across social groups

**E. Policy translation impact (GPS/WAVES context, FY23 data)**

### P1-IDEA-EEFB-0000000005 | Modeling Feedback Loops in Coupled Ecology-Economy Systems

**Created**: 2026-05-27 | **Updated**: 2026-05-27
**Priority**: P1 | **Tags**: `#ecosystem-services` `#cge` `#integrated-assessment` `#system-dynamics` `#feedback-loops` `#t-concept` `#st-new`
**Sources**:
  └ [Primary] [Conversation] Scholar (2026-05-27): Critique of Cervigni model as single-direction feedback; query about multi-loop approaches.
  └ [Primary] [File] cervigni_the_economic_case_for_nature_nca_forum_sep_2021.pdf (2026-05-27)
  └ [Secondary] [Literature] Nordhaus, W. (2017). *The Climate Casino*. Yale UP. (DICE/RICE IAM framework)
  └ [Secondary] [Literature] Meadows, D. et al. (1972/2004). *Limits to Growth*. (World3 system dynamics)
  └ [Secondary] [Literature] Corong, E. L. et al. (2017). The Standard GTAP Model, V7. *J. Global Economic Analysis*, 2(1), 1-119.

**Cite**:
- CORONG E L, et al. The standard GTAP model, version 7[J]. Journal of Global Economic Analysis, 2017, 2(1): 1-119.
- NORDHAUS W. The climate casino: risk, uncertainty, and economics for a warming world[M]. New Haven: Yale University Press, 2013.

**Note**: 引文残缺 — Meadows et al. (1972/2004) *Limits to Growth* cited with dual years, definitive edition unclear; Nordhaus *Climate Casino* publication year in original source may be imprecise (cited as 2017, standard edition is 2013).

> A conceptual taxonomy of feedback-loop structures in coupled ecology-economy models, from single-direction shocks through multi-tier closed-loop systems, and the modeling paradigms that address each level.

**Core Points**:

**1. The feedback hierarchy (ascending complexity)**

| Order | Structure | What it captures | Example |
|-------|-----------|------------------|---------|
| 0 — No feedback | ES → CGE → GDP | Exogenous shock to economy; no return path | Cervigni GTAP+ES (2021) |
| 1 — Direct feedback | ES ⇄ Land Use ⇄ CGE → GDP | Land allocation changes affect ES, which feeds back to prod. | Recursive-dynamic GTAP (GDyn-AEZ) |
| 2 — Policy/behavioral feedback | ES ⇄ CGE → GDP → Gov't Policy → ES | Human responses (subsidies, regulation, migration) become endogenous | DICE (carbon tax as control variable) |
| 3 — Full Earth-system loop | Econ → GHG → Climate → ES → CGE → Econ | Complete planetary coupling with biogeochemical cycles | Coupled IAM + ESMs (e.g., MESSAGEix-GLOBIOM) |

**2. Five modeling paradigms reviewed**

*Paradigm A — Static/RD CGE + ES (e.g., Cervigni GTAP+ES)*
- **Feedback**: 0–1 (one-way shock or recursive but calibrated)
- **Economic core**: Walrasian general equilibrium, CES production functions
- **ES module**: Offline; 4 services (pollination, timber, fisheries, carbon) as productivity shocks
- **Closed loop?** No — ES changes do not feed back into land allocation drivers
- **Best for**: Comparative statics, "what if X collapses" scenario communication
- **Limitation**: No tipping points, no adaptation, no behavioral response
- **Extension path**: Replace exogenous collapse with GTAP-AEZ land-use → ES response function

*Paradigm B — Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs), e.g., DICE/RICE (Nordhaus)*
- **Feedback**: 2 (climate → economy via damage function)
- **Economic core**: Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans optimal growth (single representative consumer)
- **ES module**: None — "nature" reduced to a temperature → GDP damage function
- **Closed loop?** Yes, but only for carbon cycle. No biodiversity, no land competition, no pollination/fisheries
- **Best for**: Climate policy optimization (social cost of carbon, optimal carbon tax)
- **Limitation**: Ecologically impoverished — one damage function cannot represent 4 ES
- **Extension direction**: Multi-sector damage functions; add land heterogeneity via GTAP-AEZ coupling

*Paradigm C — System Dynamics (e.g., World3 — Limits to Growth)*
- **Feedback**: 3 (multi-loop, non-linear, with thresholds and delays)
- **Core**: Stock-and-flow differential equations (no optimization, no equilibrium)
- **ES/Core**: Population, agriculture, industrial output, non-renewable resources, persistent pollution
- **Closed loop?** Yes, fully — including industrial output → pollution → agriculture productivity → population
- **Best for**: Exploring non-linear regime shifts, policy resistance, "what if we delay" scenarios
- **Limitation**: Ad-hoc parameterization; no micro-foundation; hard to calibrate to real economies
- **Extension direction**: Hybridize with CGE for economic block; couple with spatially explicit ES models

*Paradigm D — Coupled Earth System + Economic Models, e.g., MESSAGEix-GLOBIOM (IIASA)*
- **Feedback**: 2–3 (partial climate loop via land-energy competition)
- **Core**: Partial equilibrium (GLOBIOM) + energy system optimization (MESSAGEix)
- **ES module**: Land-use competition, forestry, bioenergy, crop yields (with climate impacts)
- **Closed loop?** Partial — land → climate → land loop exists; but marine/biodiversity ES absent
- **Best for**: Energy-land-climate nexus; IPCC scenario analysis
- **Limitation**: Massive complexity; very few institutions can maintain it; each ES addition is years of work
- **Extension direction**: Add trophic-level ecological modules (fisheries, pollination) via simplified response surfaces

*Paradigm E — Agent-Based + CGE Hybrid (frontier, no mature framework)*
- **Feedback**: 2–3 with heterogeneous agents
- **Core**: Multiple agent types (farmers, firms, governments) with bounded rationality and strategic interaction
- **ES module**: Modular; can incorporate any spatially explicit ES if linked
- **Closed loop?** Potentially yes, but technically challenging
- **Best for**: Political economy questions — why optimal policies are not adopted (winners vs losers)
- **Limitation**: Computational; no standard software; calibration is research-grade (not off-the-shelf)
- **Extension direction**: Use CGE to generate market-clearing prices, ABM for policy adoption decisions, ES model for ecological dynamics

**3. Key technical challenges across all paradigms**

| Challenge | Description | Why hard |
|-----------|-------------|----------|
| **Tipping points** | ES degradation is often non-linear: coral reef collapse, forest dieback, pollinator extinction thresholds | CGE assumes smooth substitution; system dynamics can handle it but calibration is speculative |
| **Spatial scale mismatch** | ES operate at grid-cell/local scale; CGE operates at national/regional | Downscaling CGE results → grid and upscaling grid ES → national requires strong assumptions |
| **Temporal scale mismatch** | ES degradation → recovery can be decadal/centurial; economic decisions are quarterly/yearly | Intertemporal optimization with long-lag ecological state variables is computationally heavy |
| **Commensurability** | How to put a monetary value on pollinator existence or biodiversity resilience? | Damage functions needed but deeply uncertain outside of marginal changes |
| **Political economy endogeneity** | Optimal policy ≠ adopted policy because of concentrated losses vs diffuse gains | CGE can compute welfare distribution, but policy adoption is a collective action problem, not an optimization |
| **Model coupling stability** | Two independently valid models can produce nonsense when coupled | Numerical instability, feedback loops amplifying errors, different time step requirements |

**4. Which paradigm for which question?**

| If you want to ask... | Use... | Why |
|----------------------|--------|-----|
| "What's the GDP impact if pollinators disappear?" | Static CGE + ES (A) | Counterfactual comparison; transparency matters more than dynamics |
| "What is the socially optimal carbon tax?" | IAM like DICE (B) | Built for intertemporal cost-benefit optimization |
| "Could we cross a tipping point that collapses the global food system?" | System Dynamics (C) | Non-linearity, delays, and threshold behavior are native features |
| "What does a net-zero 2050 land-energy system look like?" | MESSAGEix-GLOBIOM (D) | Sector-level detail with climate feedback baked in |
| "Why do governments not adopt P7 even though it's optimal?" | ABM or Political Economy (E) | Distributional conflict and bargaining, not social welfare maximization |
| "Does combining subsidy reform + R&D + forest-carbon payment produce more or less than the sum of parts?" | Any with policy scenario capability (A, B, D) | Standard policy package comparison; mechanism matters less than consistent accounting |

## § Time × Space × Feedback (2026-05-27 addendum)

*(This section emerged from Scholar's observation: feedback loops inherently have temporal and spatial structure; the modeling paradigms above must be evaluated along these axes too.)*

### 5. The Time Dimension: Why Feedback Speed Determines Model Choice

Every feedback loop has a **characteristic time constant** ($\tau$). The ratio $\tau_{\text{econ}} / \tau_{\text{eco}}$ determines what kind of model can capture it:

| Regime | $\tau_{\text{econ}} / \tau_{\text{eco}}$ | What happens | Handled by |
|--------|------------------------------------------|--------------|------------|
| Fast ecology ($\tau_{\text{eco}} \ll \tau_{\text{econ}}$) | ≪ 1 | Shock propagates instantly; economy reaches new equilibrium in one step | Static CGE (A) — fine |
| Similar timescales ($\tau_{\text{eco}} \approx \tau_{\text{econ}}$) | ≈ 1 | Co-evolution: ecology and economy reshape each other year by year | Recursive-dynamic CGE (A extended) or SD (C) |
| Slow ecology ($\tau_{\text{eco}} \gg \tau_{\text{econ}}$) | ≫ 1 | Ecological degradation accumulates silently; economy adapts to a slowly shifting baseline. **Most models miss this.** | SD (C) or IAM (B) with long time horizons |
| Threshold/collapse | Variable, abrupt | Ecology crosses a tipping point; $\tau_{\text{eco}}$ goes from ≫1 to ≪1 instantly | SD (C) natively; CGE cannot represent without exogenous shock |

**Example — three timescales in one feedback loop:**

- **Deforestation** → reduced rainfall → reduced crop yields (τ ≈ 5–10 years, regional)
- Farmer sees lower yields → clears **more** forest to compensate (τ ≈ 1 year, local) — **perverse feedback**
- Global carbon cycle impact (τ ≈ 50–100 years)

A static CGE captures the final equilibrium but misses the **pathological oscillatory dynamics**: deforestation → drying → more deforestation → more drying → eventual collapse. Only a model with explicit time-stepping and stock dynamics (SD or recursive CGE) can show that middle section.

### 6. The Space Dimension: Why Spatial Scale Determines Model Choice

Each feedback loop also has a **spatial extent ($R$)**. The ratio $R_{\text{shock}} / R_{\text{model}}$ determines whether the model can represent it:

| Spatial regime | $R$ of feedback | Example | Model needed |
|----------------|----------------|---------|--------------|
| Local (field/watershed) | ≤ 10² km² | Pollinator decline → crop yield ↓ → farmer abandons land → habitat loss → pollinator further ↓ | Grid-cell/landscape models (not CGE) |
| Regional (basin/country) | 10⁴–10⁶ km² | Amazon drying → reduced hydropower → economic loss → less forest protection → more drying | CGE with subnational AEZ or regional IAM |
| Interregional (trade-linked) | Global | Crop failure in one breadbasket → world food prices → land conversion elsewhere → global ES change | CGE with bilateral trade (A, D) — CGE's native strength |
| Earth-system | Planetary | Amazon + Congo + boreal forest dieback → carbon cycle disruption → global climate → all regions | Earth System Model + CGE coupled |

**Critical insight — spatial leakage:** A well-intentioned policy in one region can **displace** ecological damage to another. E.g., forest conservation in Brazil (P3-style payment) → soy production shifts to Cerrado or Africa → net global ES loss. CGE with bilateral trade **can** capture this; static single-region models cannot.

### 7. Time × Space × Feedback Cube

The three dimensions interact. A complete model classification lives in a 3D space:

| Paradigm | Time handling | Space handling | Feedback depth |
|----------|-------------|---------------|----------------|
| Static CGE + ES (A) | Comparative statics | 141 nat'l regions | 0–1 |
| Recursive-dynamic CGE (A+) | Annual steps | 141 regions × AEZ | 1 |
| DICE/RICE IAM (B) | 10-yr steps, 300 yr horizon | 1 global or 12 regions | 2 (climate only) |
| World3 SD (C) | Continuous, 0.1-yr steps | 1 global (aggregate) | 3 |
| MESSAGEix-GLOBIOM (D) | 5–10 yr steps, 2100 | 14 regions × grid cells (0.5°) | 2–3 (land-climate only) |
| ABM + CGE (E) | Annual/event-based | Heterogeneous agents × regions | 2–3, agent-specific |

**Blank spots in the cube — where no paradigm adequately sits:**

| Missing cell | Why it matters | What it would need |
|-------------|----------------|-------------------|
| High-resolution spatial × fast time × full Earth loop | Land-use displacement + climate + biodiversity feedback at farmer scale | Computationally infeasible today (exascale maybe?) |
| Slow ecology × multi-region × 3-loop | Permafrost carbon release, forest migration, species range shifts under changing economics | Needs centuries-long CGE + dynamic vegetation models + pure rates of time preference assumptions |
| Fine spatial × political economy × ES feedback | Local resistance to global optimal policies; community-level conservation outcomes | Needs ABM of land users + regional CGE + ES at watershed resolution |

### 8. Two Worked Examples

**Example A: Amazon tipping point (time × space × feedback)**

- **Initiation**: Local deforestation exceeds 20–25% of original forest cover → **savannization threshold**
- **Temporal**: Crossed over ~30 years of cumulative clearing; the rainfall decline becomes self-reinforcing after crossing threshold
- **Spatial**: Eastern Amazon dries first; reduced moisture transport affects Andean and La Plata Basin agriculture — **hundreds of km away**
- **Feedback chain**:
  1. Deforestation → reduced evapotranspiration → less rainfall (5–10 yr lag, local → regional)
  2. Reduced rainfall → crop yield decline → economic loss → less capacity for forest protection — OR → farmer clears more to compensate (1 yr, local, perverse)
  3. Forest loss → carbon release → global warming → further drying (decadal, global)
  4. Global food price rise → land conversion elsewhere → global ES loss (annual, interregional via trade)
- **Model mapping**: No single paradigm captures all four loops. Hybrid: SD for tipping dynamics + CGE for trade + ABM for farmer land-use decisions.

**Example B: Fishery collapse and policy response (time lags × spatial leakage)**

- Fishery A: Overfishing → catch declines → price rises → incentive to fish more (perverse, immediate, local)
- Management response: Catch quota → fishermen shift to Fishery B (spatial leakage, 1–2 yr, regional)
- Fishery B collapse → new quota → fishermen exit → welfare loss → government subsidy → more boats (policy delay 3–5 yr)
- **Why static CGE fails**: The oscillation pattern (boom → overfishing → collapse → subsidy → overcapacity → next collapse) is a dynamical system property, not a comparative statics result. SD captures the oscillations; CGE with recursive dynamics captures the trade linkage; ABM captures heterogeneous fishermen behavior.

**A. Context & framework**
- **Definition**: "Actions to protect, sustainably manage, and restore natural or modified ecosystems that address societal challenges effectively and adaptively, simultaneously providing human well-being and biodiversity benefits"
- **Urban resilience definition**: "Capacity of a city's systems, businesses, institutions, communities, and individuals to survive, adapt, and thrive, no matter what chronic stresses and acute shocks they experience"

- **IFC Biodiversity Finance classification**: NBS must (1) provide infrastructure-type service material to project operation, (2) displace or complement gray infrastructure

- **Key drivers of biodiversity loss addressed**: land/sea use change, pollution, overexploitation of resources, invasive species, climate change

**B. 8 NBS benefits vs. gray infrastructure**
1. **Business case**: Lower investment & O&M costs; lower CapEx replacement
2. **Climate mitigation**: Lower carbon footprint; carbon sequestration
3. **Climate adaptation**: Disaster risk reduction; infrastructure resilience
4. **Biodiversity**: Higher plant/animal diversity; improved ES
5. **Economic**: Job creation; local economic opportunities
6. **Social/health**: Cultural, educational, recreational; improved wellness
7. **Food security**: Resilience to shocks for food availability
8. **Water security**: Safeguarding sustainable water access

2. Specialized engineering expertise needed
3. Adaptive management & performance monitoring gap
4. Large land footprint for some NBS
5. Knowledge gaps: frameworks, case studies, ES valuation lacking in emerging markets
6. Permitting: lack of government guidance for nascent NBS
7. **Value for money**: Financing difficult — quantifying commercial value of NBS
8. Stakeholder consultation complexity in urban contexts
9. Long-term project visibility: impact may only appear years later

5. Adopt multistakeholder & interdisciplinary approach

> Integrated modeling of global land-use dynamics (MAgPIE + SEALS + LPJmL) downscaled to 300m resolution shows that carbon-focused restoration and protected areas alone are insufficient to reverse landscape homogenization and pollinator/soil loss; a minimum 20% semi-natural habitat in farmed landscapes is needed, achievable without additional carbon losses or productivity reductions.

**Core Points**:

**A. Paper metadata**
- **Journal**: *Nature Communications* (2023), 14, 2515 — open access
- **Lead institution**: Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research (PIK), Germany
- **Co-authors include**: Justin Andrew Johnson (same author as Cervigni/WB Earth-economy model), Hermann Lotze-Campen, Alexander Popp
- **Key relevance**: Directly addresses spatial scale mismatch in global ES modeling (our EEFB discussion), uses open-source tools (alternative to GTAP/GEMPACK)

**B. Modeling framework — MAgPIE + SEALS + LPJmL**

| Component | Type | Resolution | Function |
|-----------|------|-----------|----------|
| **MAgPIE** v4.3.5 | Global land-system model (open source) | 0.5° (~55 km) | Recursive dynamic cost optimization; allocates production to meet food/feed/bioenergy demand under biophysical and socio-economic constraints until 2100 |
| **LPJmL** | Dynamic global vegetation, crop & hydrology model | 0.5° | Simulates carbon stocks, water availability, crop yields, irrigation requirements; drives MAgPIE biophysical inputs |
| **SEALS** | Spatial downscaling model (post-processing) | **10 arc-seconds (~300 m)** | Downscales MAgPIE land-cover from 0.5° → 300m based on adjacency relationships, physical suitability (SOC + terrain), and conversion eligibility |
| **GloSEM** | Global Soil Erosion Modelling platform | 300 m | Estimates soil loss by water erosion using C-factor from crop patterns + land cover |

- **Key methodological contribution**: Coupling global (0.5°) and field-scale (300m) resolution to assess landscape-level NCP impacts — addresses the spatial scale mismatch identified in [[P1-IDEA-EEFB-0000000005]]
- **MAgPIE cost types**: Factor requirements (capital, labor, fertilizer), land conversion, transportation, investment in productivity (technological change), irrigation
- **MAgPIE spatial structure**: 12 model regions (large economies individual, smaller grouped); input data clustered per Dietrich et al.; suitability from Zabel et al.
- **SEALS initialization**: ESA-CCI land cover 2015 (37 classes → 7 functional types); adjacency strength for each type accounting for distance + agglomeration
- **Model validation**: MAgPIE validated against historical database for most outputs; SEALS empirically calibrated via loss function comparing outputs to observed fine-scale patterns; GloSEM cross-compared with empirical data + sensitivity analysis

**C. Four scenarios**

| Scenario | Area-based conservation | Climate policy (reforestation + carbon price) | Landscape policy (≥20% semi-nat. habitat) |
|----------|----------------------|---------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **BAU** (SSP2) | — | — | — |
| **PROTECT** | ✓ (+1.83B ha protected by 2030 in BH/IFL) | — | — |
| **COACTION** | ✓ | ✓ (1.5°C-consistent, NDC reforestation, +7EJ/yr bioenergy) | — |
| **MULTI** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (≥20% semi-natural habitat in farmed landscapes by 2030) |

- BAU: moderate population/income growth; current national conservation policies
- PROTECT: protected land from ~15% → ~30% of total land area (1.83B ha added)
- COACTION: 196 Mha forest restoration (still below 500 Mha feasible boundary); bioenergy demand 7 EJ/yr
- MULTI: semi-natural habitat = forest, non-forest, or grassland maintaining native species diversity

**D. Key quantitative findings**

**D1. Land use & productivity**
- Global food demand +35% (2015–2050); total land-based commodities +54%
- SSA food demand +123%; MENA +65%; India +56%
- BAU cropland expansion: **+439 Mha**; COACTION/MULTI reduce expansion by >⅔
- PROTECT only slightly slows cropland expansion (leakage effects)
- Annual crop yield increases: 1.6% (1995–2015) → drops to 0.8% (BAU/PROTECT), 1.3% (COACTION/MULTI)
- **MULTI landscape policy does not cause additional cropland expansion, carbon loss, or productivity decline** — robust to trade liberalization, investment costs, and carbon price variations

**D2. Carbon (NCP 4)**
- BAU: annual net carbon losses barely change 2015–2050
- PROTECT: −34% carbon losses vs BAU (but only −6% natural land loss difference — leakage)
- COACTION: land system becomes **net carbon sink** (233 Mt CO₂/yr uptake by 2050)
- MULTI: 223 Mt CO₂/yr — only marginally different from COACTION
- Forest restoration: 196 Mha (COACTION), 195 Mha (MULTI) — well below 500 Mha feasible limit

**D3. Landscape heterogeneity & pollination (NCP 2)**
- 2015 baseline: 399 Mha (24%) of global cropland in homogeneous grid-cells (fraction 0.8–1)
- BAU: **+66%** homogenized area (→662 Mha) — worst outcome
- PROTECT: +33% increase
- COACTION: +13% increase
- **MULTI: No increase** — all cropland fractions remain ≤0.8
- Historic cropland losing highest pollination class: 51 Mha (BAU), 50 Mha (PROTECT), 34 Mha (COACTION), **marginal net reduction (MULTI)**
- Global demand for pollinator-mediated crops: **5.2 → 7.0 EJ/yr (+34%)** ; SSA +222.5%, MENA +84.7%, India +62.9%
- **75% of crop species depend on biotic pollination**; $235–577B crop output at risk (low-income countries most vulnerable)

**D4. Soil loss by water erosion (NCP 8)**
- 2015 baseline: **44 Pg/yr** total; ~50% from cropland
- BAU: **+21 Pg/yr** increase by 2050 (highest)
- COACTION: −16 Pg/yr vs BAU (strong synergy — smaller cropland + higher second-gen bioenergy cover)
- PROTECT: only −3 Pg/yr vs BAU (limited synergy)
- **MULTI**: slightly higher overall soil loss than COACTION (cropland relocation to erosion-prone areas), but **considerably reduced soil loss in historical cropland**
- Highest regional soil loss changes: Latin America, Asia, SSA, USA
- High synergy regions for soil + climate: Sub-Saharan Africa, China (only marginal increases in COACTION/MULTI)
- **Recommendation**: Soil conservation practices (cover crops, reduced tillage, agroforestry) needed to offset relocation effects

**E. Methodological limitations (directly relevant to feedback discussion)**
- **Feedback not included**: "we could not consider feedbacks related to changes in NCP supply here, such as crop productivity changes as a result of mismatches between pollination demand and supply or due to soil loss" — explicitly identified as "promising opportunities for future research"
- Climate impacts on crop yields/water availability not considered
- Semi-natural habitat quality simplistically characterized (grassland, forest, non-forest)
- No edge effects on carbon stocks considered
- Second-gen bioenergy landscape effects only in soil loss, not pollination
- Social/political/cultural enabling conditions not modeled

**F. Policy implications**
- Area-based conservation alone (PROTECT) insufficient due to **leakage effects** — land conversion displaced to non-protected areas
- Carbon-focused restoration alone (COACTION) improves macro land-use but still allows **landscape homogenization within farmed areas**
- **Combined action (MULTI)** = biodiversity targets + climate targets + landscape heterogeneity + NCP supply can be simultaneously achieved
- 20% semi-natural habitat target does not conflict with food production if cropland is **spatially relocated** rather than expanded
- Implementation requires: (1) preventing cropland expansion into ecologically sensitive areas, (2) compensating distributional consequences, (3) landscape-context-specific management
- **Recommendation**: Landscape policy must be embedded in broader sustainable transformation framework (not pursued in isolation)

**G. Connection to research thread**
- Co-author Justin Andrew Johnson links this paper directly to the **Cervigni Earth-economy model** ([[P1-ACAD-EEM-0000000003]]) and **GPS program** ([[P1-IDEA-NCCA-0000000007]])
- **MAgPIE is open source** (github.com/magpiemodel/magpie) — a concrete alternative to proprietary GTAP/GEMPACK for land-use modeling (see [[P1-IDEA-CGET-0000000006]])
- Addresses **spatial scale mismatch** identified in [[P1-IDEA-EEFB-0000000005]] via MAgPIE→SEALS downscaling (0.5° → 300m)
- Explicitly identifies the **feedback gap** (NCP→crop productivity loop) as future research — same issue raised in our feedback loop analysis

### P1-IDEA-RNCM-000000000B | Ramsey 增长模型 + 自然资本扩展：三要素生产、反馈闭合与替代性谱系

**Created**: 2026-05-27 | **Updated**: 2026-05-27
**Priority**: P1 | **Tags**: `#ramsey` `#economic-growth` `#natural-capital` `#substitutability` `#feedback-loops` `#sustainability` `#t-concept` `#st-new`
**Sources**:

  └ [Secondary] [Literature] Ramsey, F.P. (1928). A Mathematical Theory of Saving. *Economic Journal*, 38(152), 543-559.
  └ [Secondary] [Literature] Solow, R.M. (1956). A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 70(1), 65-94.
  └ [Secondary] [Literature] Hartwick, J.M. (1977). Intergenerational Equity and the Investing of Rents from Exhaustible Resources. *AER*, 67(5), 972-974.
  └ [Secondary] [File] GPS_World Bank_NCA and Valuation of ES to Inform Policies-CFM July 2024.pdf — CWON exploring limited substitutability
  └ [Secondary] [File] cervigni_the_economic_case_for_nature_nca_forum_sep_2021.pdf — single-direction ES→CGE model
  └ [File] s41467-023-38043-1.pdf — von Jeetze et al. 2023 (MAgPIE-SEALS, feedback explicitly identified as missing)

**Cite**:
- RAMSEY F P. A mathematical theory of saving[J]. Economic Journal, 1928, 38(152): 543-559.
- SOLOW R M. A contribution to the theory of economic growth[J]. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1956, 70(1): 65-94.
- HARTWICK J M. Intergenerational equity and the investing of rents from exhaustible resources[J]. American Economic Review, 1977, 67(5): 972-974.

**Note**: 引文残缺 — Hartwick citation uses journal abbreviation "AER" in original source; GB format requires full journal name (American Economic Review).

> Theoretical synthesis: extending the Ramsey growth model from two-factor (K, L) to three-factor (K, L, R) production with natural capital, opening the substitutability question, and formalizing the feedback closure problem that connects to the broader ecology-economy modeling discussion.

**Core Points**:

**A. Standard Ramsey model skeleton**

The two core equations of the canonical Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans model:

**Production function (intensive form)**:
$$y_t = f(k_t) = k_t^\alpha, \quad 0 < \alpha < 1$$
where $y_t = Y_t/(A_t L_t)$ and $k_t = K_t/(A_t L_t)$. Constant returns to scale in $K$ and $L$: $\alpha + (1-\alpha) = 1$.

**Capital accumulation**:
$$k_{t+1} = k_t + f(k_t) - c_t - (\delta + n + g)k_t$$
where $\delta$ = depreciation rate, $n$ = population growth, $g$ = technology growth.

**Equilibrium (Euler equation)**:
$$u'(c_t) = \beta u'(c_{t+1})\big[1 + f'(k_{t+1}) - \delta\big]$$

These three equations close the system — $k_t$ determines $y_t$, $y_t$ funds $c_t$ and investment, investment becomes $k_{t+1}$, and the Euler equation ensures intertemporal optimality. The system is **closed**: no external variable enters without a counterpart equation.

**B. Adding natural capital R as a third factor**

Extending the production function:
$$y_t = f(k_t, r_t) = k_t^\alpha \, r_t^\gamma, \quad \alpha + \gamma < 1$$
(or more generally: $Y_t = F(K_t, L_t, R_t)$ where $R_t$ = natural capital stock or ecosystem service flow)

This extension immediately raises structural questions:

**B1. Returns to scale with three factors**

If we assume CRS across all three: $\alpha_K + \alpha_L + \alpha_R = 1$, then $\alpha_L = 1 - \alpha_K - \alpha_R$.

But there is a **conceptual ambiguity**: is $R$ a factor of production in the same sense as $K$ and $L$? Unlike capital and labor:
- $R$ is often **non-rival** in its benefits (e.g., climate regulation)
- $R$ is **not produced** by foregone consumption (at least not in the short run)
- $R$ enters production differently: as a flow (pollination services this season) or as a stock (soil depth)
- Some R services have **threshold effects** — below a certain stock, the service disappears entirely

**B2. The substitutability spectrum**

The most critical parameter is the **elasticity of substitution** $\sigma$ between $K_m$ (manufactured capital) and $K_n$ (natural capital):

| $\sigma_{K_m, K_n}$ | Label | Implication | Representative view |
|---------------------|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| $\infty$ | Perfect substitutes | Natural capital can be fully replaced by machines; no sustainability constraint | Standard neoclassical growth (implicit) |
| $= 1$ | Cobb-Douglas | Natural capital share of income = $\gamma$; declining $R$ reduces output but can be partially compensated | Many IAMs (implicit) |
| $0 < \sigma < 1$ | **Limited substitutability** | Below some $R$ threshold, compensation becomes increasingly costly | **CWON exploring this** |
| $\sigma = 0$ | Leontief / zero substitution | Certain natural capital services are **essential**: no substitutes exist for pollination, climate regulation | Strong sustainability (Daly, Costanza) |

The CWON (Changing Wealth of Nations, [[P1-IDEA-NCCA-0000000007]]) is actively exploring this "limited substitutability" space — a departure from traditional growth accounting that assumed perfect substitution.

**C. Modified capital accumulation with natural capital**

**C1. Three-capital taxonomy**

A complete model distinguishes at least three capital types:

| Capital | Symbol | Characteristics | Depreciation | Measurable? |
|---------|--------|---------------|-------------|-------------|
| Manufactured | $K_m$ | Produced, rival, excludable | $\delta_m$ (standard rate) | ✅ Yes, national accounts |
| Human | $H$ | Accumulated via education & health | $\delta_h$ (slow, ~2%/yr) | ✅ Approximated |
| Natural | $K_n$ | Endowed, partially non-rival, non-excludable | $\delta_n$ **implicit, nonlinear** | ⚠️ SEEA, CWON, debated |

**C2. Capital accumulation with K_n feedback**

Extend the standard law of motion:
$$K_{m,t+1} = K_{m,t} + (Y_t - C_t) - \delta_m K_{m,t}$$

$$K_{n,t+1} = K_{n,t} - D(K_{m,t}, L_t, Y_t) + \Psi(K_{n,t})$$

where:
- $D(\cdot)$ = degradation of natural capital from economic activity (the feedback from CGE → ES)
- $\Psi(\cdot)$ = natural regeneration (often nonlinear — slow at low stocks, can collapse at thresholds)
- $D$ and $\Psi$ operate at **very different timescales** (see EEFB-5 §5)

This can be written in a more compact form:
$$K_{n,t+1} - K_{n,t} = -D_t + \Psi(K_{n,t})$$
where net investment in natural capital can be negative (depletion), positive (restoration), or zero.

**The key insight**: standard national accounts record $I_{m,t} = Y_t - C_t$ as investment (increasing $K_m$), but **do not subtract** $D_t$ as depreciation of $K_n$. This is the "blind spot in economic policy" that GPS/WAVES ([[P1-IDEA-NCCA-0000000007]]) identifies.

**C3. The feedback closure problem**

The system is open if $R_t$ enters $Y_t$ but $R_{t+1}$ has no equation tying it back to $Y_t$ or $K_t$:

```
Cervigni:   K_m, L → Y → K_{m,t+1}
            R → Y (exogenous shock)
            R_{t+1} is NOT determined by the model
```

To close the loop, we need:
$$R_{t+1} = H(R_t, D(K_{m,t}, L_t), \Psi(R_t), \text{policy}_t)$$

This is precisely what von Jeetze et al. (2023) identify as missing in their MAgPIE-SEALS framework: "we could not consider feedbacks related to changes in NCP supply" (see [[P1-ACAD-MSPL-0000000009]] §E).

**D. Modeling paradigms compared against the Ramsey+Natural Capital framework**

| Paradigm | $Y = F(K, L, R)$ | $K_{n,t+1}$ closure | Euler equation | $\sigma_{K_m, K_n}$ |
|----------|-----------------|-------------------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Standard Ramsey** | $R$ absent | N/A | Yes | N/A (no R) |
| **Cervigni/GTAP+ES** | Yes (exogenous R shock) | No ($R$ set as scenario) | No (static CGE) | User-specified (CES) |
| **DICE/RICE (IAM)** | $R$ = climate damage function | Carbon cycle only | Yes (Ramsey) | $\sigma = 1$ (implicit) |
| **MAgPIE-SEALS** | Yes (via LPJmL land/R inputs) | Partial (land competition, but no R→Y feedback) | No (cost min, not utility max) | Not specified (cost-driven) |
| **World3 (SD)** | Full feedback loops | Yes (stock-flow) | No | $\sigma \approx 0$ for critical resources |
| **Full Ramsey + K_n** (frontier) | Yes | Yes (both $K_m$ and $K_n$ state equations) | **Two Euler equations?** | Key unknown parameter |

**E. Open questions and research frontier**

1. **Dual Euler problem**: With two state variables $K_m$ and $K_n$, the optimality condition requires two Euler equations — one for $u'(c_t)$ and one for the shadow price of $K_n$. The interaction between these generates complex dynamics, especially with different time preferences for natural vs. manufactured capital.

2. **Thresholds and irreversibility**: If $K_n$ can cross a tipping point (e.g., forest dieback, pollinator extinction), the function $D(\cdot)$ becomes highly nonlinear and $K_{n,t+1}$ may have "bad" equilibria. Standard Ramsey assumes smooth, convex production — this breaks down.

3. **Measuring $K_n$**: The CWON and SEEA frameworks are making progress, but current estimates of $K_n$ are partial — they cover timber, fisheries, and some minerals, but not pollination services, soil biodiversity, or climate regulation as produced capital.

4. **Policy implications of $\sigma$**:
   - If $\sigma = \infty$ (perfect substitutes): Hartwick rule works — reinvest resource rents in $K_m$, consumption never falls
   - If $\sigma < \infty$: **genuine savings** (World Bank's adjusted net savings) must be positive to sustain welfare
   - If $\sigma = 0$ for some essential services: **safe minimum standards** or **planetary boundaries** approach needed — markets alone cannot solve this

5. **From theory to modeling**: The full system requires solving:
   ```
   max ∫ u(c_t) e^{-ρt} dt
   s.t.
   Y_t = F(K_{m,t}, L_t, K_{n,t})
   K_{m,t+1} = K_{m,t} + Y_t - C_t - δ_m K_{m,t}
   K_{n,t+1} = K_{n,t} - D(K_{m,t}, L_t, Y_t) + Ψ(K_{n,t})
   R_t ≤ K_{n,t} (ecosystem service flow as function of natural capital stock)
   ```
   This is a **two-state optimal control problem** with nonlinear constraints — solvable in theory but requires:
   - Empirical estimates of $D(\cdot)$ and $\Psi(\cdot)$ (the ecological production functions)
   - Calibration of $\sigma_{K_m, K_n}$ (the substitutability parameter)
   - Numerical methods (dynamic programming or collocation) rather than closed-form solutions

**F. Connection to the broader knowledge base**

- Formally expresses the **open-loop problem** that began the EEFB-5 discussion

- The computational challenge of solving this maps to the paradigm comparison in [[P1-IDEA-EEFB-0000000005]]
- The three-capital taxonomy refines the GTAP-alternative discussion in [[P1-IDEA-CGET-0000000006]]
